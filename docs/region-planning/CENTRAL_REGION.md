# Central Region Planning Notes

This document defines the intended starting region before its material is copied
into data files, tile descriptions, faction records, rumors, and narrator
context. The current world map should be treated as draft geography. Strong
names, factions, mechanics, and lore may be salvaged, but the central region
should be rebuilt around one dominant capital with enough scale to anchor the
game.

For the capital's district-level planning, see `WHITEMARCH_CITY.md`. For the
first playable city skeleton, see `WHITEMARCH_FIRST_PASS_TILES.md`.
For the larger concern that city districts, castles, walls, and markets may need
new map/exploration mechanics, see `MAP_EXPLORATION_REWORK.md`.

## Working Identity

Working region name: **The March Basin**

Working capital name: **Whitemarch**

Both names may change later. Whitemarch is currently the strongest salvage
candidate because the existing lore already associates it with iron, roads, tax
power, and military order. If renamed, the replacement should keep the same
weight: a capital that sounds old, hard, legalistic, and rich enough that people
curse it while still walking toward it.

## Core Premise

The central region is not a gentle tutorial zone. It is the safest and most
organized part of the known world because it has the largest walls, deepest
granaries, strongest army, busiest markets, strictest law, and most developed
roads. It is safer than the wilds in the way a prison, a fortress, or a counting
house is safer than a forest.

The capital is a medieval megacity-fortress. It is militarily superior to its
neighbors, but it is not expansionist. It controls roads, bridges, tolls,
charters, debt, permits, grain, ransom, contracts, and neutral meeting ground.
It does not need to conquer every borderland because every borderland eventually
needs its markets.

The city is the central stabilizer of the playable world. It keeps trade moving,
keeps monsters away from the main roads, suppresses banditry near the basin, and
prevents smaller lords from burning each other too openly. People gather there
because the alternatives are hunger, predation, exposure, raiders, debt without
recourse, or local tyrants without appeal.

## Why People Gather Here

Whitemarch should feel inevitable. A traveler does not arrive there because it
is friendly. They arrive because the map makes the city useful.

- It holds the main river crossing and the safest stone bridges for many leagues.
- It sits near fertile grain country that can feed a huge population.
- It controls iron, timber, charcoal, and the foundries needed to arm armies.
- It has walls thick enough to shelter merchants, pilgrims, refugees, nobles,
  witnesses, debtors, fugitives, and foreign envoys.
- Its courts can enforce contracts across several cultures, at least when paid.
- Its markets are large enough to turn strange goods into coin.
- Its roads are patrolled enough that caravans prefer them despite the tolls.
- Its granaries can outlast a bad harvest or a siege.
- Its docks and caravan yards let river trade and overland trade meet.
- Its army can punish raiders, rogue lords, and predatory beasts that threaten
  the basin.
- Its neutrality is profitable. Rivals can hate Whitemarch and still need to
  negotiate inside its walls.

## Why It Does Not Expand

Whitemarch is strong enough to conquer more land, but conquest is not its main
instinct. Its ruling class profits from being the hinge of the world rather than
the owner of every field.

Its policy is containment, leverage, and punitive force:

- Keep the capital impregnable.
- Keep the basin fed.
- Keep trade roads open.
- Keep hostile powers divided.
- Keep border lords dependent on charters, loans, grain, and iron.
- Keep foreign merchants paying tolls.
- Keep enough military strength that an enemy must think in decades, not raids.
- Strike hard when deterrence fails, then withdraw once the route, hostage, or
  indemnity is secured.

Whitemarch does not occupy every neighboring culture because occupation is
expensive, garrisons rot, and distant peoples are harder to tax than to bind by
need. It prefers treaties, hostages, customs houses, debt, marriage, religious
licenses, and road forts. A conquered province is a burden. A dependent neighbor
is income.

This makes the capital resented but durable. Many powers want it checked. Few
can afford to see it fall.

## Dragon Deterrence

The central capital must answer an obvious question: why do dragons not simply
fly in, raze it, and feed?

The answer is not that dragons are weak. The answer is that Whitemarch is one of
the few cities in the world built to make dragon attack costly.

City defenses include:

- Triple curtain walls with broad kill zones between major wards.
- Stone, tile, slate, and plaster fire codes in wealthy and military districts.
- Firebreak avenues wide enough to stop roof-flame from crossing whole wards.
- Cisterns, pump houses, bucket chains, and ward-level fire watches.
- High towers with signal mirrors, bells, horns, and night beacons.
- Heavy scorpions, torsion ballistae, chain-shot engines, and roof-mounted
  harpoon frames.
- Dragon-watch companies trained to target wings, eyes, belly, and throat.
- Arsenal vaults holding relic bolts, cold-iron heads, alchemical frost jars,
  and old sainted engines that are expensive enough to be used only in crisis.
- River chains and tower nets that deny easy low passes along the water.
- Sheltered mustering yards where cavalry and infantry can survive the first
  pass and counterattack when the beast descends.
- Archive records of dragon habits, tribute routes, nesting seasons, and past
  assaults.

A dragon can kill hundreds. A great dragon can burn a district. But a direct
attack risks maiming, blindness, torn wings, broken tribute networks, rival
dragons exploiting the weakness, and the permanent loss of aura that comes from
failing to break the city.

Intelligent dragons prefer tribute, cattle, isolated towns, caravans, mountain
passes, hostage bargains, and terror that costs little. Whitemarch is not immune
to dragons. It is expensive prey.

## Strategic Geography

The central region should not use danger rings. Danger is directional,
ecological, political, and historical.

Whitemarch sits in a basin of farms, rivers, roads, and low defensive hills. The
main routes are safe by local standards because they are watched, taxed, and
maintained. Off-road travel becomes dangerous quickly, not because of distance
from the capital alone, but because patrol logic, terrain, and local power end.

Suggested layout:

- **The Capital Core:** Whitemarch and its immediate walls, docks, markets, and
  surrounding military roads.
- **The Bread Fields:** grain villages, manor farms, mills, granaries, ditch
  roads, tithe barns, threshing yards, and watch posts.
- **The Iron Belt:** quarries, charcoal camps, smithing towns, foundries,
  cart-roads, polluted streams, labor barracks, and guild checkpoints.
- **The River March:** docks, ferries, toll bridges, fishers, reed beds,
  warehouses, smuggler cuts, and floodplain villages.
- **The Old Lowlands:** marsh, plague hamlets, abandoned ditches, old battle
  cairns, drowned shrines, and ruined suburbs swallowed by wet ground.
- **The Road Inns:** fortified inns, horse exchanges, shrine-stops, customs
  sheds, patrol barracks, and small markets along the great roads.

Directional pressures:

- **North:** cold road, direwolves, hard passes, tribute towns, old war tracks,
  and Frostmaw dragons. This is the nearest clear reality check direction.
- **East:** a plural civilizational field of courts, steppe powers, island or
  peninsula realms, mountain states, treaty ports, and unequal wars.
- **South:** richer roads, royal courts, debtor markets, old slave routes,
  religious pressure, plantation or estate wealth, and hotter supply burdens.
  Southern traffic feeds Whitemarch's markets, but slavery is not a southern
  import alone; the capital keeps its own registries, courts, work gangs, and
  sale yards.
- **West:** old forests, witch courts, elven borders, fae bargains, hidden
  villages, roads that move by custom rather than survey.
- **Below:** sewers, cisterns, buried streets, old tombs, plague pits, guild
  vaults, prisons, and sealed city layers under Whitemarch itself.

## Capital Scale

Whitemarch should eventually be one of the largest handcrafted structures in the
game. A first implementation can be smaller, but the design target is a playable
city of roughly 40 to 60 meaningful hexes, grouped into districts.

Every tile should be a vantage, not a whole district compressed into one
description. The city should use the existing multi-tile structure doctrine:
gates, yards, halls, streets, interiors, courts, and buried spaces are navigated
through the map.

The city should have visible nested defenses:

- Outer farms and road forts.
- Outer wall and gate wards.
- Trade wards and common wards.
- Inner wall around the high city or citadel.
- Arsenal and barracks zones with restricted access.
- Palace, council, or governor's keep.
- Underlayers of sewers, cisterns, and old-city remains.

## Districts Of Whitemarch

The following districts are content targets, not necessarily all first-pass
tiles. Each should eventually include several walkable vantages.

### Crown Gate

The main landward entrance. Caravan queues, inspection sheds, toll clerks, armed
porters, road dust, beggars, money changers, and soldiers checking papers. New
players should feel the city before entering it: bells, walls, smoke, shouted
numbers, and the smell of animals waiting too long.

Possible tiles:

- Outer approach road.
- Gate killing ground.
- Toll hall.
- Inspection yard.
- Watch barracks.
- First market under the wall.

### Caravan Yards

Walled storage yards where merchants sleep near their goods. Camel strings,
mule trains, ox carts, foreign drivers, mercenary guards, teamsters, wheelwrights,
grain weighers, and hired scribes. Violence here is bad for business and punished
fast.

Possible tiles:

- Eastern yard.
- Western yard.
- Weigh house.
- Wagon repair shed.
- Guard hiring board.
- Foreign drovers' camp.

### Grand Market

The largest public market in the central region. Food, iron, cloth, spices,
medicine, salvage, animals, rumors, licenses, false papers, stolen goods hidden
among legal ones. The market should feel crowded enough that the player can get
lost in ordinary human pressure.

Possible tiles:

- Grain square.
- Butchers' row.
- Cloth awnings.
- Coin scales.
- Auction stones.
- Night market alleys.

### The Chain Market

Whitemarch's public slave market, built where the Grand Market, River Docks, and
Bond Registry can all touch it without admitting they are the same machine.
Sales here are legal spectacle: status criers, court clerks, guild buyers,
quartermasters, estate factors, foreign brokers, collar-smiths, temple
petitioners, translators, guards, and families trying to prove a paper was
misfiled before a name is called.

The market does not only sell people brought from the south. It receives:

- War captives taken in punitive expeditions.
- Beast folk and non-human prisoners bought from border lords.
- Criminals converted to labor by sentence.
- Tax-default villagers seized as household or estate collateral.
- Debt-bound workers sold onward when a creditor calls the note.
- Unchartered foreigners whose protection papers fail inspection.
- Captives from northern raids, western forest conflicts, eastern border
  skirmishes, and southern slave routes.
- Children of enslaved households whose status is inherited through city law.

The Chain Market is regulated rather than hidden. It has posted tariffs,
inspection bells, city guards, guild booths, physicians paid to assess work
value, and registry clerks who can make a person disappear into correct ink.
Public sales happen by daylight. Private viewings happen behind screens and in
covered yards. The city's respectable houses do not need to visit often; their
agents know the schedule.

Possible tiles:

- Public sale steps.
- Covered viewing yard.
- Status criers' platform.
- Collar stalls.
- Physician's inspection booth.
- Guild buyers' arcade.
- Family petition rail.
- Guarded holding cells.
- Broker counting room.
- Drain gate used by escape-runners.

### River Docks

A second city pressed against the water. Barges, cranes, river chains, fish
markets, warehouses, customs officers, smugglers, ferrymen, mud, rope, tar,
shouted manifests, and bodies sometimes found in the pilings.

Possible tiles:

- High quay.
- Low fish dock.
- Customs house.
- River chain tower.
- Warehouse row.
- Smuggler stairs.

### Smithing And Arsenal Quarter

The loudest ward. Foundries, armorers, arrow mills, bellows, charcoal smoke,
guild marks, weapon proofs, military orders, and guarded gates into the state
arsenal. This is where the city's dragon deterrence becomes visible.

Possible tiles:

- Public smith row.
- Foundry court.
- Guild proofing yard.
- Arsenal gate.
- Siege engine shed.
- Relic bolt vault approach.

### Guildhall Row

The city's legal economy in stone. Charters, monopolies, apprentice rolls,
disputes, work permits, price fixing, bribes, and ceremonies of craft pride.
Guilds are powerful enough to bargain with nobles and cruel enough to starve
unlicensed workers.

Possible tiles:

- Mercers' hall.
- Smiths' hall.
- Teamsters' hall.
- Scribes' hall.
- Guild court.
- Apprentice dormitory lane.

### Bond Registry And Labor Yards

The offices, yards, pens, dormitories, and courtrooms through which coerced
labor is recorded, priced, transferred, punished, leased, and inherited. The
registry turns domination into paperwork.

The Bond Registry is the bureaucratic face of the Chain Market. It decides who
is protected, who is transferable, who is inheritable, who is temporarily bound,
who is state property, who belongs to a guild, and who may be leased by the day
to docks, foundries, road crews, mines, kitchens, noble houses, temple hospitals,
or military quartermasters.

The city's labor categories should be ugly and precise:

- Chartered citizens are protected from chattel bondage but not from debt,
  prison labor, conscription, or workhouse sentence.
- Resident foreigners have partial rights that depend on papers, patrons, and
  treaties.
- Unchartered humans, refugees, conquered peoples, and outsiders from disfavored
  subraces can fall into debt bondage, penal labor, forced apprenticeship, or
  sale by court judgment.
- Non-human peoples are often denied full legal personhood unless protected by
  charter, embassy, temple, or powerful patron.
- Beast folk and peoples marked as monstrous are openly collared, leased,
  branded, and assigned to dangerous work.
- War captives, criminals, and tax-default villages feed labor gangs.
- Debt bonds are often recorded as temporary and lived as permanent.

This system is one reason Whitemarch is hard to topple. Its walls, roads,
quarries, docks, and arsenals are fed by forced labor, and every powerful
institution has a ledger proving its share.

The Registry's power is procedural:

- It can reclassify a foreigner's status when a treaty lapses.
- It can convert a debt into labor after missed payments.
- It can attach a whole household to an estate's obligation.
- It can mark a non-human as property unless a charter says otherwise.
- It can sell the labor term of a prisoner before the prisoner is tried.
- It can recognize manumission papers, delay them, lose them, or price them out
  of reach.
- It can lease state slaves to guilds and then blame guilds for whatever happens
  in the yard.

Possible tiles:

- Registry hall.
- Auction court.
- Collar smith.
- Debt court.
- Labor yard.
- Barrack row.
- Quartermaster lease office.
- Quiet alley used by escape networks.

### Quarry Pens And State Work Gangs

Not all slavery is sold in market view. The state keeps labor columns for wall
repair, road stone, ditch clearing, river dredging, sewer work, corpse hauling,
firebreak demolition, and emergency military works. These gangs are marched
through side gates before dawn and counted back under torchlight.

The quarry pens sit outside the clean districts but inside the city's military
reach. They are where Whitemarch turns bodies into stone, lime, brick, timber,
and road metal.

Possible tiles:

- Lime kiln.
- Road-stone yard.
- Chain barrack.
- Overseer shed.
- Punishment post.
- Water trough.
- Hidden message wall.

## Surrounding Settlements

The capital should not float alone. The central region needs secondary places
that depend on it while keeping their own local texture.

### The Drowned Inn

Keep as a memorable starting place, but reposition it as a fortified road inn
within the capital's economic gravity. It sits near marsh road, toll road, or
old causeway, far enough from the walls to feel exposed and close enough that
Whitemarch patrols, merchants, and rumors pass through.

The inn is where the player first hears the region's logic:

- North road warnings.
- City toll gossip.
- Bond registry rumors.
- Caravan hiring talk.
- Patrol presence.
- Smugglers using old marsh paths.
- Refugees choosing between hunger and Whitemarch law.

### Bridgeforts

Small forts guarding river crossings. Each has soldiers, toll clerks, stables,
chain towers, and local resentment. Bridgeforts make Whitemarch's power visible
outside the walls.

### Grain Villages

Protected, taxed, watched. Villages feed the city and receive patrols in return.
Their people may resent both bandits and tax wagons.

### Smith Towns

Dependent towns in the Iron Belt. Smoke, guild control, labor barracks, tool
markets, weapon testing, charcoal hunger, and poisoned streams.

### Manor Estates

Noble-controlled land around the basin. Private guards, tenant obligations,
marriage politics, hunting rights, old grudges, and peasants who know exactly
which lord will not protect them.

### Marsh Edge Hamlets

Half-protected places where the capital's map begins to fail. Fisherfolk,
reedcutters, smugglers, fever, old ditches, drowned shrines, and people who
prefer uncertain marsh law to certain city law.

## Central Factions

These are proposed faction seeds for later data work.

### Wall Command

Professional soldiers responsible for gates, walls, alarm drills, dragon-watch
towers, siege engines, and emergency musters. They are respected, feared, and
politically dangerous because no ruler controls the city without them.

### Road Wardens

Patrol force securing major roads, inns, and bridgeforts. They escort tax wagons,
hunt bandits, inspect corpses after raids, and sometimes decide which villages
are worth arriving in time for.

### The Iron Guilds

Smiths, foundry masters, armorers, teamsters, charcoal factors, and weapon
proofers. They claim craft honor and practice economic strangulation.

### The Grain Office

Granary bureaucracy. Keeps famine away when competent, creates famine when
corrupt. Its ledgers can start riots or end sieges.

### The Bond Registry

Status courts, labor leases, collar records, debt rolls, and prison work orders.
Hated by many, useful to every institution with money.

The Registry is not a single counter. It is a ministry with court officers,
field inspectors, archive clerks, approved brokers, seal-bearers, and armed
retrieval men. Its official title is **The Office of Bonds, Status, and Civic
Labor**, but most people call it the Bond Registry or the Ink Chain.

Internal branches:

- **Status Court:** rules whether a person is citizen, protected resident,
  foreigner, ward, debtor, slave, criminal laborer, or disputed property.
- **Lease Office:** rents state-owned and court-held slaves to guilds, docks,
  estates, temples, and military crews.
- **Collar Archive:** records marks, brands, collar numbers, household descent,
  manumissions, transfers, and fugitives.
- **Recovery Writs:** armed officers and bounty contractors sent after escaped
  slaves, vanished debtors, and contested persons.
- **Auction Sealers:** certify sales at the Chain Market and private yards.

### Chain Factors

Licensed slave brokers who operate between the Chain Market, estate houses,
military quartermasters, southern caravans, border lords, and foreign sellers.
They are not a formal guild because Whitemarch prefers the fiction that the
Registry controls the trade, but the largest factors behave like a guild in all
but name.

Known factor houses can be added later. They should be wealthy, hated, protected,
and socially present in respectable wards through donations, marriages, loans,
and temple endowments.

### Collar-Smiths

Specialist smiths licensed to make collars, tags, chain-rivets, transport
locks, work gang irons, and numbered plates. They belong to the iron economy but
answer to Registry inspection. Their shops sit near the Chain Market and the
Smithing Quarter, and their apprentices learn early which orders are never
entered under a noble's real name.

### Flesh Wardens

Street name for the Registry's recovery officers and market guards. They carry
city writs, hooked staves, hounds where allowed, and enough legal authority to
drag a person across ward boundaries while everyone nearby suddenly remembers
urgent business elsewhere.

### The Charter Courts

Judges, clerks, advocates, and legal patrons who define personhood, contract,
inheritance, debt, citizenship, and foreign rights.

### Merchant Syndics

Powerful merchant houses that profit from neutrality, tolls, credit, insurance,
ransom, and war supply.

### Licensed Temples

Faith institutions tolerated by the city because they heal, bury, bless oaths,
calm crowds, educate clerks, and sometimes spy.

### Undercity Networks

Smugglers, thieves, hidden priests, escaped laborers, counterfeiters, sewer
guides, corpse carriers, and those who know the gates beneath the gates.

Important branches:

- Escape-runners who move people through drains, carts, funeral traffic, and
  foreign compounds.
- Paper-forgers who specialize in manumission, safe-conduct, guild tokens, and
  temple protection letters.
- Safehouse keepers in the slums, docks, and temple hospitals.
- Dock crews who can hide a person in cargo if paid or persuaded.
- Former slaves who know the market schedules, collar marks, and which guards
  drink on duty.

### Foreign Compounds

Embassies, protected trade houses, hostage delegations, and cultural enclaves.
They should be practical points of contact for later regions, especially the
eastern macro-region.

## Military Doctrine

Whitemarch is strong because its violence is institutional.

Military strengths:

- Heavy infantry for walls and streets.
- Crossbow and arbalest companies.
- Engineers and siege crews.
- Dragon-watch crews.
- Road cavalry for patrol and pursuit.
- River chain guards.
- Licensed mercenary companies under city contract.
- Arsenal reserves.
- Emergency citizen musters by ward.
- Hostage towers to control noble and foreign compliance.

Military weaknesses:

- Slow to act outside prepared routes.
- Politically divided command.
- Reliant on grain and pay.
- Corruption in supply contracts.
- Heavy forces struggle in marsh, deep forest, mountains, and steppe.
- Non-expansionist doctrine can look like cowardice to aggressive neighbors.
- Internal riots can pin the army inside the walls.

This supports believable warnings: the capital can defeat many threats in a
prepared place, but it cannot make every direction safe.

## Starting Experience

The player should begin near, but not at the center of, Whitemarch.

Recommended start:

- The Drowned Inn sits on a marshy road within the capital's trade orbit.
- Travelers are moving toward and away from Whitemarch.
- The first rumors establish that the capital is nearby, huge, useful, and
  unpleasant.
- The first choices can involve road work, escort jobs, toll trouble, inn debt,
  missing laborers, a caravan hiring guard, a city clerk seeking witnesses, or a
  patrol refusing to enter a marsh hamlet.
- The city should be reachable early, but entering its deeper districts should
  require papers, money, purpose, or a willingness to be searched.

The starting region's promise: civilization is close enough to save you and
strong enough to devour you.

## Directional Warning Examples

NPC warnings should be practical local knowledge rather than visible level
labels.

North:

## Tone Targets For Descriptors

Use sensory detail that makes power feel material:

- Stone sweating in fog.
- Bell towers and counting bells.
- Smoke from foundries.
- River mud and tar.
- Grain dust in sunbeams.
- Iron filings, blood, hot oil, wet wool.
- Crowds speaking over each other.
- Clerks scraping quills.
- Chains, not as decoration, but as labor sound.
- Walls large enough to make weather feel smaller.
- Patrol boots at night.
- Firebreak avenues like scars through dense wards.
- Dragon engines under oiled covers.

Avoid making Whitemarch gleaming or purely rotten. It should be both magnificent
and exhausting.

## Early Quest And Encounter Seeds

- A caravan master hires guards because the official road patrol is delayed by
  a court order.
- A debt court clerk needs a witness found before a judgment turns permanent.
- A beast-folk laborer escapes from a quarry lease and several factions want
  them for different reasons.
- A family at the Chain Market claims the person on the sale steps was born
  chartered, but the Registry seal says otherwise.
- A Chain Factor's private viewing yard is robbed, and the missing captives are
  worth enough that the city treats the theft like treason.
- A Collar-Smith apprentice brings proof that a noble house ordered unregistered
  irons.
- A Flesh Warden drags the wrong foreigner from the docks and two embassies turn
  the case into a street confrontation.
- A manumission archive burns the night before several long-held debt bonds
  would have expired.
- A granary rat hunt reveals a hidden tunnel into an old plague vault.
- A dragon-watch relic bolt has gone missing from the arsenal inventory.
- A village refuses a grain tithe because last year's patrol never came.
- A noble's hostage child vanishes from a guarded tower.
- A foreign merchant's safe-conduct is declared forged after their goods are
  already impounded.
- A road inn is accused of sheltering smugglers through marsh paths.
- A guild apprentice dies in a foundry accident that was not an accident.
- A river chain jams before a foreign barge convoy arrives.
- A northern survivor reaches the gate frostbitten, carrying proof that a
  Frostmaw dragon has shifted territory.
- An execution square speech turns into a riot when bread prices are announced.
- A temple hospital hides someone whose legal status would send them to the
  registry.
- A sewer guide offers passage under a closed gate and refuses to say who built
  the old tunnel.

## Implementation Translation Notes

When this document is converted into game data:

- `biomes.js` should give the central basin enough space to breathe.
- Existing major powers should be moved farther apart if they crowd the start.
- `factions.js` should add central institutions before adding more distant
  empires.
- Slavery-facing factions should include the Office of Bonds, Chain Factors,
  Collar-Smiths, Flesh Wardens, and escape networks as practical city powers.
- `handcrafted-tiles.js` should build Whitemarch as a multi-tile city, not a
  single POI.
- `rumored.js` should carry directional warnings and near-region landmarks.
- `spawn-tables.js` and biome `extraSpawns` should distinguish roads, farms,
  city wards, marsh edges, and undercity threats.
- Starting location should keep the capital nearby but not collapse the whole
  region into the first room.
- City interiors, restricted compounds, prisons, and undercity routes should
  use `doors` where access control matters.

The central region should be complete enough that a player can remain there for
a long time: working, trading, getting entangled, entering the city, leaving it,
returning changed, and understanding why everyone else also comes back.
